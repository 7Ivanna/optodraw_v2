## Options

```python
scene.set_view_layout({"3d": {"spacing": "auto", "clearance": 5.0}})
```

- `{"3d": {"spacing": 3.0}}`: three times the distance between part centres in 3D; `"auto"` makes room from
  each part's footprint. The 2D drawing does not change.
- `{"1d": {"spacing": 1.0}}`: the 1D schematic follows the 2D positions instead of its compact automatic
  layout; a bench with loops, like this one, reads better that way.
- `scene.preview(layout=...)`: the same setting for one preview only.
- `Beam(a, b, c, wavelength=...)`: light through parts in order; the colour follows the wavelength.
- `Beam((a, "out"), (b, "in"))`: through named ports, the place light really enters a part.
- Beams on one path nest on the axis by themselves; `offset=0.7` draws a lane.
- `Note.on(beam, "880 mW", leg=3)`: text beside one leg of a beam.
- `scene.lens_beam(...)`: the lenses set the width.
- `part.label_side`: the name above, below, at a side or at a corner of the part.
- `part.beam_height`: the height of that part's beam in 3D; the periscopes before it follow.

## What it supports

Lenses, mirrors (flat and curved), splitters and dichroics, wave plates and polarizers, filters,
objectives, fibres, cameras and detectors, stages, posts and periscopes. The
[part catalogue](/reference/parts/) lists every one.

## How it works

Each part draws itself in 2D, 1D and 3D from one set of parameters. A beam is a list of anchors; a part's
ports give the exact point light enters and leaves. For physics, [trace the rays](/ray-tracing/).
Automatic 3D spacing reads footprints only; it does not check the 3D bodies for contact.
